Measuring Visible Lines in the Hydrogen Spectrum
2025, v.0.1.7
In this experiment, students use light dispersion and diffraction to mea-
sure the wavelengths of spectral lines. Reliable results require care-
ful calibration of the device, and the basic principles of calibration are
briefly introduced. The data analysis involves propagating measure-
ment uncertainties and applying weighted regression to obtain accurate
wavelength values. The experiment shows how simple measurements
can reveal the quantum nature of matter.
Introduction
When matter is heated or exposed to external energy sources such as
electric discharge or cathode rays, it begins to emit electromagnetic
radiation. This radiation is not uniform but consists of waves of dif-
ferent wavelengths, forming what is known as aspectrum. Spectra
can be studied by using dispersion and diffraction, which separate the
radiation into its wavelength components.
Different states of matter produce different types of spectra. Hot
solids and liquids emit radiation across almost all wavelengths, form-
ing acontinuous spectrum— seen as a smooth band of colors from
red to violet. Heated gases and vapors, in contrast, produce either
band spectra(broad colored bands separated by dark gaps) orline
spectra(sharp, discrete lines). Thus, the type of spectrum depends
strongly on the physical state of the substance.
In solids, atoms are arranged in a rigid lattice due to strong in-
teractions. In liquids and amorphous solids, interactions are weaker
because of greater atomic motion. In gases and vapors, atoms and
molecules are far less bound, so their emission spectra consist of dis-
tinct lines that directly reflect the internal structure of the atoms.
The hydrogen atom, being the simplest of all, provides the clearest
example of such a line spectrum. Its study opened the way to mod-
ern atomic physics and serves as the foundation for this laboratory
experiment.

Bohr’s Theory of the Hydrogen Atom
Early models treated atoms as tiny oscillators that emit electromag-
netic waves when their charges move. While this idea could explain
some optical effects, it could not predict the specific frequencies ob-
served in atomic spectra. Experiments showed that the charge-to-mass
ratio of the “oscillating particle” matched that of the electron, suggest-
ing that light emission is tied to the motion of electrons inside atoms.
Ernest Rutherford’s experiments withα-particle scattering on gold
foil led him to propose aplanetary modelof the atom: a dense, pos-
itively charged nucleus at the center with electrons orbiting around it
(Figure1).

tum mechanics.
Spectral Analysis
Experiments show that many elements have spectral regularities sim-
ilar to hydrogen. They also form series of spectral lines, but the pat-
terns are usually more complex. According to quantum mechanics,
these regularities arise because each atom has a unique set of discrete
energy states. The arrangement of these states determines the posi-
tions of spectral lines, making every element’s spectrum unique —
essentially a “fingerprint” of the atom.
This uniqueness makes spectroscopy a powerful tool forqualitative
spectral analysis.By comparing observed spectral lines with known
reference values, one can identify the chemical elements present in a
sample.
To study spectra, physicists use special instruments: spectroscopes
– for direct visual observation of spectral lines, spectrographs – which
